# Three Protocol Choices Shape Measured Fidelity of AI Inpainting on Museum Paintings

*Manuscript draft v3*

## Abstract

Full-reference benchmarks of off-the-shelf image inpainting on digitized paintings are sensitive to three pipeline choices: latent-space mask downsampling, hole prefill color before denoising, and safety-filter denominator accounting. We quantify these choices and evaluate four fixed models—LaMa, MAT, PowerPaint v1, and SDXL Inpainting—on 120 open-access paintings from the Cleveland Museum of Art and the Art Institute of Chicago across four medium-and-support groups and six procedural losses, using crops taken without further resampling from each museum's published rendition. Default nearest-neighbor latent downsampling misses a mean of 59.5% of 1%-crack pixels across the 60 Cleveland works; grid alignment cuts PowerPaint v1 masked mean absolute error (MAE) by about 45% across 40 complete paired works (safety checker on) while lowering its scorable output rate. At SDXL's model-card example strength, changing hole prefill color alone shifts overall center-masked MAE by 0.0725 (with larger shifts on broad losses) and reverses luminance bias; pairing ring-color prefill with those example settings cuts Cleveland SDXL MAE by nearly two-thirds yet leaves a wide gap above non-diffusion baselines. PowerPaint's safety checker withholds 21.9% of Cleveland conditions versus 2.8% in Chicago. Under the controlled protocol, LaMa ≈ MAT < PowerPaint v1 < SDXL (within our practical margin for LaMa and MAT) held identically across both museums, all four media, and all six losses; these choices altered error magnitudes and the scored sample without reversing the ordering. In these cohorts, the best-performing model did not depend on painting medium, whereas reporting these three choices is essential for reproducible comparison.

*Keywords*: image inpainting · digital painting restoration · evaluation protocols · museum digitizations · full-reference metrics · latent diffusion

## 1. Introduction

Digital image inpainting offers a fast way to visualize completions for losses in digitized paintings, from fine craquelure and paint flaking to broad lacunae. In cultural heritage contexts, however, visual plausibility alone is insufficient. Conservation ethics treat loss compensation as a reversible, detectable, and documented intervention grounded in respect for original material [@icomcc2008; @aic1994], a principle recently demonstrated in physical practice through reversible digitally printed masks [@kachkine2025]. When digital inpainting supports documentation, visualization, or restoration planning, the central technical question is reconstruction fidelity: how closely a model recovers missing local color, brushwork, and structure rather than inventing plausible but unfaithful content. Because authentic historical losses lack surviving ground truth, controlled evaluation relies on masking intact museum digitizations and scoring predicted pixels directly against the recorded artwork using automatic full-reference distances.

Existing painting-inpainting studies have compared neural models under simulated or transferred losses, yet three low-level protocol choices in modern pipelines can substantially alter measured fidelity and the scored cohort (Figure 1). First, the tested SDXL Inpainting and PowerPaint pipelines downsample pixel-space binary masks by a factor of eight using nearest-neighbor interpolation [@diffuserssdxl0352; @powerpaintv1src]. Subsampling onto an 8-pixel grid can omit narrow structures from explicit mask conditioning, a broader issue in latent-space inpainting [@shamsolmoali2025tdpaint]; on 1% painting cracks and flakes, it drops over half of the intended hole pixels. Second, when SDXL runs with denoising strength below 1.0, as in its model card's 0.99 example [@sdxlinpaint], the pipeline encodes the pre-masked input image into the initial noisy latent [@diffuserssdxl0352]. Because ground-truth hole pixels must be erased prior to encoding, the replacement color used to prefill the hole can affect generated outputs—a qualitative sensitivity discussed in Diffusers development [@diffusers8450], whose quantitative luminance and error effects on paintings we measure here. Third, bundled safety checkers can withhold generated outputs; scoring only returned images compares models on different, pipeline-filtered subsets whose size varies sharply across collections. Alongside these protocol gaps, prior multi-model benchmarks either aggregate across heterogeneous artworks [@hu2025imagest] or stratify by visual subject such as portraits and landscapes [@controlled300], leaving practitioners uncertain whether model rankings shift across distinct physical media and supports such as oil on canvas, oil on wood, watercolor on paper, and ink on paper.

In this study, we quantify the effects of all three protocol choices and benchmark four fixed off-the-shelf inpainting models—two non-diffusion architectures (LaMa [@suvorov2021] and MAT [@li2022mat]) and two latent diffusion models (PowerPaint v1 [@zhuang2023] and SDXL Inpainting [@sdxlinpaint])—under a pre-specified protocol, committed to version control before each inference run (Figure 2). Across 120 open-access paintings equally balanced across four medium-and-support groups from the Cleveland Museum of Art (60 works) and an independently sampled replication cohort from the Art Institute of Chicago (60 works), we extract 512 × 512 crops taken without further resampling from each museum's published rendition and apply six shared procedural loss conditions spanning cracks, scattered flakes, and contiguous losses at 1% and 5% area fractions. We isolate latent-grid alignment through a full 60-work Cleveland paired comparison supported by our earlier 16-work pilot, isolate prefill color through a univariate three-color comparison (black, mid-gray, surrounding ring mean) plus a full-cohort SDXL sensitivity arm, and characterize safety-filter attrition through planned-denominator tracking and a conditional replay of all withheld Cleveland cases.

Across two museum cohorts and targeted diagnostic, prefill, and replay experiments, our study makes four main contributions:

1. **Quantification of latent-space mask under-coverage and alignment tradeoffs.** Across the Cleveland cohort, default nearest-neighbor latent downsampling omits a mean of 59.5% of narrow crack pixels; aligning masks to the latent grid lowers PowerPaint v1 masked MAE by about 45% on complete paired works while reducing its scorable output rate, whereas SDXL shows mixed condition-dependent shifts.
2. **Quantification of prefill-color sensitivity under partial-strength denoising.** Fixing SDXL at the model card's 0.99 example settings and varying only the hole prefill color shifts overall center-masked MAE by 0.0725 (with larger shifts on broad losses) and reverses luminance bias; pairing surrounding-ring prefill with those example settings substantially lowers Cleveland SDXL MAE while leaving a wide gap above LaMa.
3. **Quantification of cohort-dependent safety-filter withholding.** PowerPaint v1 withholds 21.9% of planned conditions in Cleveland versus 2.8% in Chicago under identical settings, and replayed Cleveland conditions exhibit higher mean error than complete safety-on works, demonstrating why coverage denominators must accompany fidelity scores.
4. **Cross-museum, cross-medium stability of model rankings.** Under the controlled protocol, the masked-MAE ordering `LaMa ≈ MAT < PowerPaint v1 < SDXL` holds identically across both museums, all four medium-and-support strata, and all six damage conditions (with overall LaMa–MAT differences inside our 0.01 MAE practical margin); the three protocol choices changed error magnitudes and the scored sample without reversing this ordering in our tests, whereas between-group gap magnitudes varied across collections.

## 2. Related Work

### 2.1 General-purpose image inpainting pipelines

Classical inpainting formulated hole filling as local continuation from known boundaries, propagating isophotes via partial differential equations [@bertalmio2000] or copying best-matching patches by structure-prioritized exemplar synthesis [@criminisi2004]. Deep learning shifted inpainting toward learned global priors, beginning with context encoders combining reconstruction and adversarial losses [@pathak2016] and extending to arbitrary hole geometries via partial convolutions [@liu2018] and gated convolutions [@yu2018]. To bridge wide gaps at higher resolutions, subsequent non-diffusion architectures introduced co-modulated generative adversarial networks [@zhao2021], fast Fourier convolutions with image-wide receptive fields in LaMa [@suvorov2021], and mask-aware transformer attention in MAT [@li2022mat]. Because LaMa and MAT operate directly on pixel-grid inputs without text conditioning or content filters, they provide strong non-diffusion baselines for local continuation and run deterministically under our fixed-seed configuration.

A second paradigm performs conditional generation inside the compressed latent space of an autoencoder [@rombach2021] or via iterative diffusion resampling [@lugmayr2022]. Widely used open checkpoints include SDXL Inpainting 0.1, trained at 1024 × 1024 resolution with mask and masked-image UNet conditioning channels [@sdxlinpaint]; PowerPaint v1, which adds learnable task prompts to separate context-aware filling from object insertion and removal [@zhuang2023]; dual-branch architectures such as BrushNet [@ju2024]; and large rectified-flow transformers such as FLUX.1 Fill [@fluxfill]. Moving inpainting into latent space introduces three operational factors largely absent from pixel-space networks: spatial quantization of fine masks onto an 8× downsampled latent grid [@shamsolmoali2025tdpaint; @ju2024], coupling between pre-masked RGB inputs and initial noisy latents whenever `strength < 1.0` [@diffuserssdxl0352; @diffusers8450], and safety filters bundled with public pipelines. Our study quantifies how these three pipeline choices affect full-reference fidelity and sample availability on digitized paintings.

### 2.2 Digital painting restoration, damage modeling, and benchmarks

Early computational restoration studies demonstrated virtual crack detection and inpainting on masterworks such as the Ghent Altarpiece while noting the lack of surviving ground truth for authentic damage [@cornelis2013]. Subsequent work paired domain-specific models with heritage datasets, including Dunhuang mural benchmarks using random-walk or real-damage masks [@yu2019dunhuang; @shao2023; @xu2024dunhuang], deep image prior formulations for frescoes and style-coherent filling [@merizzi2024; @morotti2024], dual-encoder networks for ancient Chinese paintings [@sun2024], super-resolution and textile restoration pipelines [@hu2024; @wang2024silk], fine-tuned diffusion for frescoes [@smolka2025], and synthetic craquelure generation [@cuchguillen2026]. In parallel, pixel-level damage segmentation datasets such as ARTeFACT catalog real analogue damage across heritage media [@ivanova2024artefact], while several restoration papers transplant real damage silhouettes onto intact Chinese paintings [@zhang2026inkrenew; @xue2025]. Creative painterly editing frameworks that insert new objects or text-guided content pursue a different goal from conservation-oriented reconstruction [@xu2023refpaint; @fanelli2024].

Four recent benchmarks are closest to our multi-model evaluation. Cipolina-Kun et al. compared CoModGAN, LaMa, and GLIDE on transformations of a single Escher lithograph plus a qualitative fresco example [@cipolinakun2022], leaving multi-artwork and cross-medium generalization untested. Imagest benchmarked a style-conditioned generator against VQGAN, Stable Diffusion 1.4/1.5, and DALL·E 2 on 100 artificially damaged paintings cropped or resized to 256 × 256 pixels, reporting aggregate full-reference metrics without medium or support stratification [@hu2025imagest]. MDTD-ArtIR evaluated blind restoration and editing models on 1,000 WikiArt paintings degraded by semi-transparent texture overlays rather than binary missing-region masks [@vijendran2026]. Most closely, Controlled-300—an unreviewed Zenodo master's research archive [@controlled300]—compared Telea, LaMa, HINT, and Stable Diffusion across 300 paintings grouped by visual content (portraits, landscapes, architecture, abstract, and high texture) alongside a 35-work fixed-area mask robustness check, reporting low masked MAE for LaMa. Relative to these precedents, we quantify latent-grid mask coverage, prefill initialization, and safety-filter denominators, stratify explicitly by physical medium and support rather than visual genre, and test replication across two independent museum open-access collections without downsampling museum renditions into uniform 256-pixel thumbnails.

### 2.3 Full-reference evaluation metrics and conservation ethics

Automatic restoration evaluation must distinguish pixel-level distortion from perceptual or structural similarity, a tension formalized at the distribution level by the perception–distortion tradeoff [@blau2018]. Pixelwise RGB error (MAE) directly measures local numeric agreement, while CIEDE2000 (`ΔE₀₀`) provides an unsigned, perceptually weighted color distance in CIELAB space [@luo2001ciede2000; @sharma2005ciede2000]. Learned full-reference metrics extend comparison to mid- and high-level representations: LPIPS aligns with human pairwise similarity judgments [@zhang2018lpips], whereas DISTS unifies structure and texture similarity with partial tolerance to stochastic texture resampling [@ding2020dists]. More recent work targets localized failure modes more specifically, including MISATO's `CLIP@mask` for masked semantic alignment and `Gradient@edge` for boundary color seams [@wang2025misato], high-level fidelity proxies in super-resolution [@rocafort2025], controllable hallucination testing in HalluGen [@kim2026hallugen], and discussions of iconographic hallucination in mural restoration [@ren2026mapgr].

Conservation charters establish why transparent, auditable protocols matter even in purely digital evaluations. The 2008 ICOM-CC terminology resolution defines restoration as actions undertaken with respect for original material [@icomcc2008], and Section 23 of the AIC Guidelines for Practice requires compensation for loss to be documented, detectable by common examination methods, and reversible [@aic1994]. Translating these norms to a computational benchmark motivates preserving exact unmasked original pixels outside the scoring hole, logging immutable inputs and outputs, and reporting all safety-filtered cases alongside full-reference scores rather than silently discarding failures.

## 3. Materials and Methods

### 3.1 Study overview and pre-specified workflow

To separate architectural differences from evaluation artifacts, we built a modular, pre-specified benchmark pipeline (Figure 2). Every experimental stage—the center-masked pilot, the 16-work native-scale pilot and latent-alignment correction, the SDXL parameter and univariate prefill studies, the 60-work Cleveland main cohort, the Cleveland review and conditional replay, the 60-work Chicago replication, and the full 60-work Cleveland original-mask comparison—was specified in a version-controlled protocol committed before GPU inference, with run windows and artifact hashes logged for verification. Beginning with the full-cohort Cleveland original-mask comparison, each run sidecar additionally recorded the executed Git commit hash, working-tree cleanliness, binary diff digest, and Python source hash at launch. All results in this study characterize reconstruction fidelity and output availability for the pinned off-the-shelf checkpoints, prompts, single-seed main settings, and 512-pixel procedural loss protocols described below. Full implementation, sampling audit, and checksum details are provided in Supplementary Information (`docs/paper/SUPPLEMENT.md`).

### 3.2 Museum cohorts, sampling, and crop preparation

We assembled two independent open-access painting cohorts of 60 artworks each (120 total), evenly balanced across four physical medium-and-support groups (15 works per group): oil on canvas, oil on wood support, watercolor on paper, and ink on paper.

- **Cleveland Museum of Art (CMA) cohort.** Sixty CC0 paintings were sampled deterministically from Cleveland's open-access collection using the museum's full-size downloadable image renditions [@cma-openaccess]. Sixteen works overlapped with our earlier native-scale pilot. One frozen crop (*The Trinity*) was uniformly black RGB `(0, 0, 0)` in the museum source file; under our pre-specified rule against post-selection replacement, it was retained in the main cohort and verified in an exploratory exclusion check.
- **Art Institute of Chicago (AIC) independent replication cohort.** Sixty public-domain paintings were sampled independently from a frozen AIC catalog export [@aic-openaccess] using deterministic SHA-256 ordering within each medium-and-support stratum, restricting selection to verified official 1686-pixel-wide IIIF JPEGs, at most one work per identifiable primary artist per group, and zero image or object overlap with Cleveland. One watercolor (`aic-114337`) painted on paper mounted onto card met the frozen catalog filter and was verified via a separate exclusion check (Supplementary Note S1 details candidate screening and exclusions).

After EXIF orientation correction and conversion of embedded color profiles to sRGB, each artwork contributed one 8-bit RGB 512 × 512 crop at a fixed pseudorandom integer coordinate, **taken without further resampling from each museum's published rendition**. Each crop received six procedural binary masks crossing three damage morphologies—a thin tortuous connected crack, dispersed small paint flakes, and a single contiguous irregular loss—with two target area fractions (1% and 5% of the 512 × 512 crop), totaling 360 planned conditions per museum. The six masks assigned to within-group ranks 0–14 were identical across all four medium-and-support groups and identical between Cleveland and Chicago, ensuring exact geometric matching across strata and collections.

### 3.3 Pinned models and the three controlled protocol experiments

We evaluated four publicly released inpainting models on a single NVIDIA RTX 3090 (24 GiB VRAM) using fixed seed `2026` in all main runs (checkpoint versions, adapter notes, and FLUX.1 Fill [@fluxfill] pre-screening are detailed in Supplementary Note S2):

- **LaMa** [@suvorov2021]: IOPaint TorchScript release, standard 512-pixel inference without iterative refinement.
- **MAT** [@li2022mat]: IOPaint Places-512 release (`truncation_psi = 1`) with deterministic per-condition latent seeding.
- **PowerPaint v1** [@zhuang2023]: pinned author release, DDIM scheduler, 512 resolution, 25 steps, classifier-free guidance (CFG) 7.5, `strength = 1.0`, positive context task token (`P_ctxt`) paired with a single generic painting-restoration prompt, negative object token (`P_obj`), and the built-in safety checker kept enabled in all main arms.
- **SDXL Inpainting 0.1** [@sdxlinpaint]: pinned Diffusers checkpoint, 9-channel UNet, EulerDiscreteScheduler, 512 resolution, the same generic painting-restoration prompt, 25 requested steps, CFG 7.5, and `strength = 1.0` in main arms.

**Protocol Control 1: Latent-space mask alignment and shared input aperture.** Both SDXL Inpainting and PowerPaint downsample binary masks by 8× via nearest-neighbor interpolation onto a 64 × 64 latent grid at 512-pixel inference [@diffuserssdxl0352; @powerpaintv1src]. In our main comparison, we enforced a shared latent-aligned aperture across all four models: every 8 × 8 pixel block intersecting the original scoring mask was marked as masked in the model input, and pixels inside this expanded mask were zeroed (black) before inference. Final compositing placed generated pixels strictly inside the original unexpanded scoring mask and preserved exact original pixels everywhere outside (verified pixel-for-pixel across all outputs). To measure the effect of mask alignment directly at full scale, we ran a pre-specified paired comparison on all 60 Cleveland works (360 conditions each for PowerPaint v1 and SDXL) feeding the unexpanded original mask directly into both diffusion pipelines while keeping all other settings fixed. We also ran original-mask input sensitivity arms for LaMa and MAT in both Cleveland and Chicago (720 runs per museum) and retained our earlier 16-work native-scale and 60-work center-masked alignment runs as supporting evidence.

**Protocol Control 2: Prefill initialization and SDXL parameter sensitivity.** In Diffusers' SDXL Inpainting pipeline [@diffuserssdxl0352], `strength = 1.0` initializes denoising from pure Gaussian noise while keeping zero-centered masked-image conditioning. When `strength < 1.0`—such as the model card's example settings of `strength = 0.99`, CFG 8.0, and 20 requested steps [@sdxlinpaint], for which the pinned Diffusers v0.35.2 pipeline executes 19 effective denoising steps [@diffuserssdxl0352]—the pipeline additionally encodes the full pre-masked RGB input through the VAE and adds noise to that latent (Supplementary Note S3 gives the Euler initialization scaling). Because ground-truth pixels must be erased before VAE encoding, the hole prefill color can directly shift generated luminance and error.

To isolate prefill color as a single variable, we fixed SDXL at 512 resolution, `strength = 0.99`, CFG 8.0, 20 requested steps, and seed `2026`, varying only the RGB color inside the expanded hole across three arms: black (RGB 0), mid-gray (RGB 128), and an 8-pixel surrounding ring mean (the single per-image rounded sRGB mean of visible pixels within an 8-pixel neighborhood strictly outside the expanded mask). This univariate test ran on both the 60 center-masked works (5% irregular central hole) and the 16 native-scale pilot works (96 conditions per color). We then evaluated the strongest configuration (`0.99 / 8.0 / 20` + ring-color prefill) across all 360 Cleveland main conditions to test whether favorable SDXL initialization closes the gap to non-diffusion baselines. Additional single-seed sensitivities on the 60 center-masked works tested 1024-pixel inference and an empty prompt. We also visually audited 12 randomly sampled Cleveland SDXL main cases via three-column contact sheets (original, LaMa, SDXL) and reproduced two cases pixel-for-pixel to verify determinism.

**Protocol Control 3: Safety-filter denominators and conditional replay.** Whenever PowerPaint's built-in safety checker withheld an output, the run was repeated once with the identical seed and inputs to check for transient runtime failures; persistent flags were recorded as withheld cases rather than scored as black images, and kept in the planned denominator. To assess how Cleveland's 79 withheld conditions (19 works) affected the four-model comparison, we audited museum catalog subject descriptions of those 19 paintings and executed a single-pass conditional replay of exactly those 79 conditions with the safety checker disabled (identical crops, masks, seed, and settings), evaluating both the 79-condition replayed subset and the combined 60-work / 360-condition mixed cohort alongside the primary safety-on cohort.

### 3.4 Metrics and statistical analysis

Our primary fidelity metric is **masked MAE**: the mean absolute RGB difference between the reconstructed and original crops computed strictly over pixels inside the original unexpanded mask, divided by 255 to lie on `[0, 1]`. Four secondary full-reference metrics capture chromatic accuracy, perceptual similarity, and texture/structure preservation without human raters:

1. **Pixel-pair mean CIEDE2000 (`ΔE₀₀`)** inside the original mask (unsigned perceptual color distance; D65 illuminant, CIE 1931 2° observer) [@luo2001ciede2000; @sharma2005ciede2000].
2. **CIEDE2000 of masked mean CIELAB colors**, an unsigned distance measuring the magnitude of net color difference between the predicted and reference hole averages without shift direction.
3. **Local LPIPS** (AlexNet v0.1) [@zhang2018lpips] on the original mask's tight bounding box padded by an 8-pixel margin (clipped to the 512 × 512 boundary).
4. **Local DISTS** [@ding2020dists] on the same padded bounding box.

For prefill analyses, we additionally computed **signed luminance bias**: the mean difference (output minus reference) in Rec. 709 weighted sRGB luminance `[0, 1]` inside the original mask, where negative values denote darkening and positive values denote brightening. For all distance metrics, lower values indicate closer agreement with the reference crop.

Within each museum, overall artwork-level scores weighted the six damage conditions equally and included only artworks with all six conditions available for the comparison at hand. Four-model summary tables therefore use the common complete-work denominator (41/60 in Cleveland, 54/60 in Chicago), whereas pairwise comparisons against LaMa retain every artwork complete for that specific model pair (60/60 for MAT and SDXL in both museums; 41 and 54 for PowerPaint). Per-condition summaries use all artworks complete across all models in that comparison. Uncertainty was quantified via 10,000 artwork-level cluster bootstrap resamples, yielding unadjusted 95% percentile confidence intervals. Before running the main experiments, we pre-specified practical equivalence margins of **0.01 masked MAE** (about 2.55 gray levels per 8-bit channel) and **0.02 local LPIPS**; medium-and-support contrasts and per-condition breakdowns are interpreted descriptively.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 Model rankings replicate identically across both museum collections

Under the controlled protocol, the four-model ordering was identical in both Cleveland and Chicago for our two thresholded metrics, masked MAE and local LPIPS: **`LaMa ≈ MAT < PowerPaint v1 < SDXL`** (Table 1). Point-estimate means followed the same sequence (LaMa, MAT, PowerPaint v1, SDXL) for pixel-pair `ΔE₀₀` and local DISTS, while MAT scored slightly lower than LaMa on unsigned mean-Lab `ΔE₀₀` in both museums. On complete four-model cohorts, LaMa achieved a masked MAE of 0.0315 in Cleveland (41 complete works, 246 conditions) and 0.0387 in Chicago (54 complete works, 324 conditions), closely matched by MAT at 0.0339 and 0.0421. ### 4.2 Latent-space mask under-coverage drops over half of thin losses and alters both error and availability

Across all 60 Cleveland works (360 planned conditions), default 8× nearest-neighbor downsampling omitted **59.54%** of scoring-mask pixels on 1% cracks and **52.93%** on 1% flakes, compared with 22.46% on 5% cracks, 25.01% on 5% flakes, 13.85% on 1% contiguous losses, and 6.63% on 5% contiguous losses (Table 3; Figure 4). ## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 Guidance for museum and digital-heritage practitioners

Our two-museum results provide a clear operational rule for practitioners selecting off-the-shelf inpainting tools for localized painting losses (1%–5% area): **model choice did not depend on painting medium or support.** Across oil on canvas, oil on wood, watercolor on paper, and ink on paper in both Cleveland and Chicago, LaMa and MAT delivered practically interchangeable full-reference fidelity (differing by at most 0.0045 MAE and 0.0123 LPIPS in stratum point estimates, with overall differences inside our 0.01 MAE and 0.02 LPIPS margins), required no prompt engineering, and achieved 100% output availability without safety-filter refusals. On fine cracks, dispersed flakes, and small lacunae, these pixel-space architectures consistently tracked local palette and surrounding texture more faithfully than the tested latent diffusion models.

When latent diffusion pipelines are considered—for example, to explore generative hypotheses over wider semantic losses—our experiments highlight two practical deployment hazards. First, default pipeline settings can severely distort local color: SDXL at `strength = 1.0` produced mean-Lab `ΔE₀₀` shifts above 26, and `strength = 0.99` with black prefill introduced substantial darkening (−0.0847 to −0.1685 luminance). Even under its strongest tested ring-color configuration across Cleveland, SDXL's masked MAE (0.1114) remained more than three times that of LaMa (0.0325). Second, built-in safety checkers can silently block large shares of museum imagery—nearly 39% of oil-on-canvas conditions in Cleveland—turning batch workflows into unpredictable subset processors unless refusal rates are monitored explicitly.

### 5.2 Reporting standards for painting-inpainting benchmarks

For researchers designing or reviewing digital restoration benchmarks, our paired comparisons show that mask alignment and SDXL initialization settings can shift masked MAE by 0.047 to 0.190—far above our 0.01 practical margin—even with identical model weights and test artworks, while safety filtering alters both the scored sample and availability (including when mask expansion itself reduces PowerPaint availability from 83.3% to 78.1%). We therefore recommend three mandatory reporting items for any full-reference painting-inpainting benchmark:

1. **Input-to-latent mask alignment and compositing boundary.** Specify how pixel masks map onto the latent grid, report the fraction of target pixels covered (or the expanded-to-original area ratio if aligned), composite generated pixels strictly inside the original unexpanded mask, and include an original-mask sensitivity check for pixel-space baselines so that aperture tradeoffs remain transparent.
2. **Pre-masking and denoising initialization.** Whenever `strength < 1.0`, verify that ground-truth pixels are erased prior to VAE encoding and state the exact prefill rule (black, mid-gray, or surrounding-ring mean). Reporting `strength = 1.0` alongside explicit prefill-color sensitivities prevents arbitrary initialization choices from being conflated with model capability.
3. **Planned denominators and refusal accounting.** Report planned, scorable, and withheld counts per model and stratum alongside complete-case fidelity scores. When safety checkers or runtime failures exclude a non-trivial share of conditions, include conditional sensitivity checks to verify whether selective attrition shifts error estimates or rankings.

Committing pre-specified sampling orders, frozen crop and mask hashes, code-provenance metadata, and practical decision margins to version control before inference further guards against inadvertent selection bias. As our Cleveland–Chicago contrast illustrates, an apparent between-medium difference in a single collection (watercolor versus oil on canvas) can shrink and lose precision upon independent replication.

### 5.3 Limitations of the study

Five scope boundaries govern our findings and point directly to future work. First, all damage masks were procedurally generated (cracks, scattered flakes, and contiguous losses at 1% and 5% area) rather than authentic historical loss contours transplanted from damaged donor paintings; authentic mask-transfer studies require sufficient medium- and support-matched donor annotations with clear provenance and reuse licenses, which remained scarce in our audit of ARTeFACT [@ivanova2024artefact]. In addition, our main runs used a single fixed inference seed (`2026`) and 512-pixel resolution across all four pinned models, whereas SDXL was trained at 1024 × 1024; although our 1024 center-masked sensitivity did not alter the ranking against LaMa, multi-seed native-1024 studies and newer rectified-flow models such as FLUX.1 Fill [@fluxfill] on higher-memory or quantized setups warrant separate investigation. Second, because 512 × 512 crops were taken without further resampling from each museum's published rendition, Cleveland's full-size files and Chicago's 1686-pixel-wide IIIF JPEGs may span different physical painting areas per pixel; physical scale standardization requires per-artwork millimeter calibration from museum imaging metadata. Third, painting media and supports naturally covary with subject matter, historical period, brushwork scale, and digitization pipelines, so between-group contrasts are descriptive rather than causal effects of material alone. Fourth, 16 of the 60 Cleveland paintings overlapped with our earlier native-scale pilot, which we mitigated through the independently sampled Chicago replication cohort while retaining two frozen edge cases (*The Trinity* black crop and Chicago's card-mounted watercolor `aic-114337`) verified via exclusion checks. Fifth, automatic full-reference pixel, color, and perceptual distances measure agreement with a digitized photograph under synthetic masking, not historical authenticity or conservation validity; physical restoration decisions remain the responsibility of trained conservators guided by archival and technical evidence [@aic1994; @icomcc2008].

---

## 6. Conclusion

Across 120 open-access paintings from two independent museums, three evaluation protocol choices—nearest-neighbor latent mask downsampling, hole prefill color under `strength < 1.0`, and safety-filter denominator accounting—substantially alter measured reconstruction error and effective sample composition in AI painting inpainting. Under the controlled protocol, the four-model masked-MAE ordering (`LaMa ≈ MAT < PowerPaint v1 < SDXL`, with LaMa and MAT within our pre-specified practical margin) was identical on matched cases across both museum collections, all four painting media and supports, and all six procedural damage conditions; these three choices changed error magnitudes and the scored sample but did not reverse the ordering in our tests. For localized losses, strong non-diffusion baselines provided a consistent choice across painting media, whereas future heritage-inpainting benchmarks must report mask alignment, prefill initialization, and coverage denominators to produce comparable, trustworthy evidence.
