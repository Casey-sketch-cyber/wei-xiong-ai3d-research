Style-induced volumetric bias in image-to-3D
reconstruction:
A controlled benchmark on artistic input transformations

Abstract
Single-image 3D reconstruction systems are increasingly used to lift visual concepts into editable assets, yet their evaluation still depends heavily on point-distance
metrics measured against ground-truth geometry. This paper studies a failure mode
that becomes visible when the same object is reconstructed from matched photographic and artistic renderings. We build a controlled benchmark from 100 curated
Objaverse objects, render each object as a photographic input and five stylised variants, and run the same TripoSR reconstruction pipeline on all 600 inputs. Although
several stylised inputs improve Chamfer distance or F-score relative to the photographic input, they consistently reduce predicted mesh volume. The mean volume
ratio relative to photo ranges from 0.257 for sketch to 0.500 for flat colour, with
Wilcoxon tests below 10−16 for all styles. Edge, low-detail, and watercolour inputs
also substantially reduce z-extent. A 30-object rerun with a higher marching-cubes
resolution preserves the same pattern, suggesting that the effect is not simply a
low-resolution mesh-extraction artefact. The results show that conventional point
metrics can reward geometrically thin or collapsed reconstructions under artistic domain shifts. We therefore argue that image-to-3D evaluation for design and assetgeneration workflows should report volumetric and thickness-oriented diagnostics
alongside Chamfer and F-score.

Keywords: image-to-3D, 3D reconstruction, artistic inputs, geometry evaluation,
volumetric bias, benchmark.

1.

Introduction

Single-image 3D reconstruction has moved from a specialised vision problem into a practical tool for design, game production, digital heritage, and AI-assisted asset generation.
Recent systems can produce a mesh from one image in seconds or minutes, and large
reconstruction models have made this workflow much easier to deploy (Hong et al., 2023;
Tochilkin et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2024). The same shift is now visible in adjacent design
tasks, including AI-assisted CAD generation and single-image reconstruction for cultural
heritage (Doris et al., 2026; G. Wu et al., 2026). In these settings, the input image is not

1

always a clean photograph. Artists and designers often begin with sketches, flat illustrations, low-detail thumbnails, stylised concept art, or watercolour-like references. A 3D
generator that appears robust on natural images may therefore behave differently when
the visual evidence is intentionally abstract.
The usual evaluation protocol does not fully expose this problem. A reconstructed
mesh can score well under Chamfer distance or F-score because its visible surface lies
close to the sampled ground-truth surface, even when the object has lost volume, thickness, or usable back-side structure. For engineering and design use, these failures matter.
A thin shell, flattened body, or collapsed depth profile may be a poor 3D asset even
if a point-cloud metric improves. The issue is sharper for artistic inputs because style
transformations often remove texture, compress shading, simplify boundaries, or emphasise contours. These changes can make the input easier for a metric that favours visible
outline alignment while making the recovered object less useful as geometry.
This mismatch is not merely a visual-quality concern. Many image-to-3D systems are
now presented as asset-generation tools rather than as point-cloud predictors. The output
may be remeshed, sculpted, textured, animated, inserted into a game engine, used as a
design reference, or passed to a fabrication-oriented workflow. In such cases, a mesh that
is topologically simple but volumetrically collapsed can be more damaging than a mesh
with modest surface noise. It may look acceptable from the source view but fail when
rotated, edited, or interpreted as a solid object. This is why an evaluation protocol that
reports only surface proximity can be misleading for computational design.
Artistic inputs make the problem unusually testable. A sketch, flat-colour rendering,
or watercolour image removes some visual cues while preserving others. Contours may
remain sharp even when shading and texture are gone; a flat illustration may retain semantic identity while suppressing depth evidence; a low-detail image may preserve global
silhouette while erasing local geometry. If the same underlying object is reconstructed
from both a photographic rendering and a stylised rendering, the paired difference can
reveal which part of the reconstruction is robust to abstraction and which part depends
on photographic cues. This paired design is stronger than comparing unrelated photographs and artworks because it fixes object identity, pose, camera view, and ground-truth
geometry.
This paper studies that mismatch through a controlled benchmark. Instead of collecting unrelated photographs and artworks, we start from known 3D objects. Each object is
rendered into a photographic reference and five matched artistic variants: edge, flat colour, low-detail, sketch, and watercolour. This paired design keeps the underlying object
fixed, keeps the 3D ground truth available, and isolates the effect of input style. We then
run the same image-to-3D model, TripoSR (Tochilkin et al., 2024), on every input and
compare each stylised reconstruction with the corresponding photo reconstruction.
The main finding is that stylised inputs can look better by conventional point metrics
while becoming worse as volumetric assets. On 100 Objaverse objects (Deitke et al.,

2

2022), every artistic style reduces predicted mesh volume relative to the photo input.
The reduction is large, with mean volume ratios of 0.332 for edge, 0.500 for flat colour,
0.305 for low-detail, 0.257 for sketch, and 0.343 for watercolour. At the same time, sketch
improves mean F-score@0.05 by 0.093 and Chamfer distance by 0.010 relative to photo.
The metric table therefore supports two different readings of the same reconstructions:
surface proximity improves, but usable volume is lost.
Our contribution is not a new image-to-3D generator. It is an evaluation and diagnostic study for image-to-3D reconstruction under artistic input shifts. We make three
contributions. First, we define a paired cross-style benchmark in which photographic and
stylised inputs share the same object and known 3D ground truth. Second, we show
that stylised inputs induce a consistent volumetric bias in TripoSR outputs, including
strong reductions in volume and z-extent. Third, we identify a metric paradox: Chamfer
distance and F-score can improve while the reconstructed mesh becomes substantially
thinner or smaller. The result suggests that evaluation for computational design and 3D
asset generation should treat volumetric diagnostics as first-class evidence rather than
optional visual checks.
The paper is organised as follows. Section 2 reviews single-image 3D reconstruction,
artistic or sketch-based 3D generation, and recent evaluation work. Section 3 describes
the paired benchmark, the reconstruction protocol, and the metric design. Section 4
reports the main 100-object experiment and the higher-resolution robustness rerun. Section 5 discusses the implications for benchmarking and design workflows, and Section 6
summarises the main limitations.

2.

Related work

2.1

Datasets and generative priors for 3D reconstruction

The recent acceleration of image-to-3D research depends on two ingredients: large 3D
object corpora and generative priors learned from 2D or multi-view data. Objaverse
made a large collection of annotated 3D objects available for training and evaluation
(Deitke et al., 2022), and Objaverse-XL expanded the scale by more than an order of
magnitude (Deitke et al., 2023). More recent curation work adds quality annotations
and filtering signals for object-level datasets (Lin et al., 2025). These resources make
it possible to build controlled benchmarks in which the image input is generated from
known 3D geometry rather than collected from unrelated web images.
This distinction matters for evaluation. Web images and artworks often differ in object
category, viewpoint, scene context, lighting, and level of abstraction. A comparison across
such images can easily confound model sensitivity to style with ordinary differences in
content. Large object corpora make a cleaner design possible: one can render the same
mesh under different visual conditions and then measure all reconstructions against the
same ground truth. The present benchmark follows this controlled-evaluation logic. It
3

uses Objaverse as a source of object geometry, but it does not treat the dataset itself
as the contribution. The contribution is the paired style manipulation and the metric
analysis that becomes possible once the object is fixed.
Large 3D corpora also shape the models being evaluated. Several recent reconstruction
models are trained on synthetic or rendered views of large object collections, so their
behaviour depends on what kinds of cues are common in those training views. If a model
has mainly learned from naturalistic renderings, it may infer depth from shading, texture
gradients, and photographic appearance. A stylised image can remove precisely those
cues. The benchmark therefore probes a realistic distribution shift: the object remains
familiar, but the evidence available to the reconstruction model changes.

2.2

Single-image 3D generation and large reconstruction models

Single-image 3D generation has moved from optimisation-heavy pipelines toward feedforward reconstruction. Zero-1-to-3 learns view-conditioned generation from one image
(R. Liu et al., 2023); One-2-3-45 and One-2-3-45++ combine view generation and reconstruction to produce meshes quickly (M. Liu, Shi et al., 2023; M. Liu, Xu et al., 2023).
Wonder3D introduced cross-domain diffusion for single-image 3D generation, and Wonder3D++ extends the same line toward higher-fidelity results (Long et al., 2023; Yang,
Long et al., 2025). Large reconstruction models such as LRM, TripoSR, InstantMesh, and
CRM further reduce inference time or improve sparse-view reconstruction quality (Hong
et al., 2023; Tochilkin et al., 2024; Z. Wang et al., 2024; Xu et al., 2024).
The field has continued to move quickly after these early large reconstruction models. MeshLRM, Unique3D, MeshFormer, GS-LRM, LRM-Zero, GeoLRM, and multi-view
LRM variants explore higher-quality mesh generation, Gaussian reconstruction, syntheticdata training, and geometry-aware positional encoding (M. Li et al., 2024; M. Liu et al.,
2024; Wei et al., 2024; K. Wu et al., 2024; Xie et al., 2024; C. Zhang et al., 2024; K. Zhang
et al., 2024). More recent work, including NOVA3D, Cue3D, MAGE, and UniMesh, shifts
attention toward normal-aligned video diffusion, image-cue attribution, material-aware 3D
generation, and joint mesh understanding/generation (Huang et al., 2026; X. Li et al.,
2025; H. Wang et al., 2025; Yang, Li et al., 2025). This rapid progress makes evaluation
more important, not less: a model can be fast and visually plausible while still failing
under input conditions that are common in design practice.
Most of these systems are evaluated on natural images, synthetic multi-view renderings, or standard object benchmarks. That evaluation is necessary, but it does not answer
whether a system is reliable for design sketches or illustrative concept images. TripoSR
is a useful case study for this question because it is fast, open, and representative of feedforward image-to-3D reconstruction. We use it as a diagnostic baseline rather than as the
object of a model-ranking study. The results should therefore be read as evidence that
the evaluation problem exists, not as a final statement about every current image-to-3D
model.

2.3

Artistic, illustrated, and sketch-based 3D inputs

Non-photographic image-to-3D input now has its own line of work. Sketch-A-Shape studies zero-shot sketch-to-3D generation (Sanghi et al., 2023), Sketch3D adds style-consistent
guidance for sketch-driven generation (Zheng et al., 2024), and PASTA uses part-aware
priors for sketch-to-3D shape generation (Lee et al., 2025). A 2026 survey on deep sketchbased 3D modelling shows that sketch-driven 3D creation has become a broad research
area rather than a single-task variant (Tono et al., 2026).
Other work studies more specialised stylised domains. PAniC-3D reconstructs stylised
anime portraits (Chen et al., 2023); DrawingSpinUp animates single character drawings
(Zhou et al., 2024); Art3D targets flat-coloured illustration with a training-free correction
pipeline (Cong et al., 2025); and a semi-automated framework for medieval manuscript
miniatures evaluates several image-to-3D systems on historical illustrated inputs (Pallotto
et al., 2026). Recent work also extends stylisation to reconstructed 3D assets and scenes,
including 3D stylisation through large reconstruction models and language-driven artistic
scene generation (Gu et al., 2025; Oztas et al., 2025).
Our study is positioned differently from these domain-specific systems. We do not
introduce a new stylised reconstruction model, nor do we claim that artistic 3D generation
is unexplored. Instead, we use matched styles of the same ground-truth objects to test
how input abstraction changes the measured geometry. The paired construction lets us
observe metric disagreement without confounding style with object category, pose, or
dataset source.
This positioning is important because specialised stylised models and general-purpose
reconstruction models answer different questions. A specialised method may be designed
to handle a particular artistic domain, such as anime portraits or flat-coloured illustrations. A general-purpose model may be used by practitioners because it is available, fast,
and easy to run. Our benchmark asks what happens in the latter case: if a user gives a
general reconstruction model a design-like input, do the standard metrics reveal the loss
of usable 3D structure? The answer in our experiment is no, or at least not reliably.

2.4

Evaluation benchmarks and the metric gap

Evaluation for generative 3D models is beginning to receive more direct attention. 3DGenBench proposes a broad benchmark suite for 3D generative models (Y. Zhang et al.,
2025), 3D Arena provides an open platform for generative 3D evaluation (Ebert, 2025),
and SI23DCQA studies perceptual quality assessment for single-image-to-3D content (Fu
et al., 2025). In computational design, CADBench similarly treats generation quality as
a benchmark problem rather than a single example-by-example judgement (Doris et al.,
2026). These efforts reflect a wider concern: as 3D generation becomes easier to run,
visual plausibility alone is not enough to certify geometric usefulness.
Chamfer distance and F-score remain useful because they are reproducible and com-

5

patible with sampled point clouds. Yet they measure surface proximity more directly than
asset usability. A reconstruction can place many sampled points close to a ground-truth
surface while losing volume, thickness, back-side plausibility, or connected structure. For
computational design and engineering workflows, the mesh is not only an image surrogate. It may be edited, remeshed, textured, animated, fabricated, or used as a proxy for
downstream analysis. A flattened mesh is therefore a different failure from a small surface offset. The present benchmark treats volume, z-extent, and mesh-complexity ratios
as diagnostic signals that complement point-distance metrics.
Recent benchmark work points in the same direction. 3DGen-Bench and 3D Arena
broaden evaluation beyond isolated visual examples, while SI23DCQA studies perceptual
quality for single-image-to-3D outputs (Ebert, 2025; Fu et al., 2025; Y. Zhang et al., 2025).
CADBench brings a similar benchmark mentality to AI-assisted design, where executability and compactness matter in addition to geometric fidelity (Doris et al., 2026). Our
work adds a narrower but practical test: when the image style changes while the object
stays fixed, conventional point metrics can disagree with volume-sensitive diagnostics.
That disagreement is precisely the kind of failure a design-oriented benchmark should
expose.

3.

Material and methods

3.1

Object selection

We use Objaverse as the source of ground-truth 3D objects (Deitke et al., 2022). From the
available objects, we curate a 100-object subset for the main experiment. The curation removes very large meshes, extremely low-complexity meshes, obvious intellectual-property
or trademarked objects, human figures, full scenes, and collection-like samples. We also
retain only permissively licensed objects marked as BY or BY-SA in the run manifest.
This keeps the benchmark small enough to reproduce while avoiding many cases where
reconstruction failure would be caused by object ambiguity rather than style.
The subset is deliberately modest. The aim is not to approximate the full diversity of
Objaverse, but to create a controlled diagnostic benchmark that can be rerun on affordable
hardware. Each selected object is treated as a unit of paired comparison. The same object
contributes one photo reconstruction and five stylised reconstructions, so the statistical
test is based on within-object changes rather than on differences between object categories.
This design reduces variance from mesh complexity, object identity, and pose. It also
makes the results easier to interpret: a volume ratio below one means that the stylised
input produced a smaller mesh than the photo input for the same object.
During curation we prioritised objects that could be rendered as recognisable singleobject inputs. Full scenes, assemblies with many disconnected parts, and objects whose
identity depended on fine text or brand marks were removed. These cases are important
in practice, but they introduce additional failure modes that are not the focus of this

3.2

Matched input styles

For each object, we render a photographic input and five stylised variants. The style set is
designed to cover common artistic abstractions while keeping the experiment controlled:
• photo: the reference input used as the paired baseline;
• edge: a contour-dominant rendering with reduced interior evidence;
• flat: a flat-colour version with simplified shading;
• low-detail: a simplified rendering with reduced visual detail;
• sketch: a hand-drawing-like abstraction;
7

• watercolour: a soft stylised rendering with diffuse boundaries and colour variation.
The important property is pairing. Each stylised input corresponds to the same underlying
mesh and view as its photo counterpart. This lets us report relative changes style by style
instead of comparing unrelated images.
The five stylised variants were chosen to cover different forms of abstraction rather than
to reproduce one named artistic school. Edge inputs preserve outline information while
discarding much of the interior appearance. Flat-colour inputs preserve object regions
but reduce shading gradients. Low-detail inputs suppress local texture and small-scale
visual evidence. Sketch inputs emphasise drawn contours and sparse strokes. Watercolour
inputs retain colour variation but soften boundaries and local contrast. These transformations are not intended to exhaust the space of artistic images. They provide a compact
set of controlled perturbations that resemble common inputs in early-stage design and
illustration workflows.
All styles share the same view as the photo rendering. This is essential because a different view would change the amount of visible geometry and make the paired comparison
ambiguous. The photo input is used as the baseline because it represents the input type
for which many image-to-3D systems are most commonly demonstrated. We do not treat
the photo reconstruction as ground truth. Ground truth remains the original 3D object.
The photo reconstruction is used only as a within-model reference for asking how much
a style transformation changes the model output.

3.3

Reconstruction protocol

All 600 inputs are reconstructed with TripoSR (Tochilkin et al., 2024). We use TripoSR
because it is a fast, open image-to-3D baseline and can process the full benchmark at
modest cost. The goal is not to rank models; it is to test whether a widely used reconstruction pipeline exhibits style-induced metric disagreement under controlled inputs.
The main run reconstructs 100 objects across six styles with the default mesh-extraction
setting used in the experiment pipeline. All 600 reconstructions succeed and produce
watertight meshes.
To check whether the observed volume reduction is merely caused by the meshextraction resolution, we run a separate 30-object robustness experiment with marchingcubes resolution increased to 128. This rerun covers the same six styles and produces 180
successful reconstructions.
The reconstruction protocol is intentionally held constant across styles. We do not
tune model parameters separately for sketch, flat, watercolour, or photo inputs. Stylespecific tuning would make it difficult to determine whether the measured differences
come from the input or from the intervention. The same pipeline therefore receives each
rendered image, produces a mesh, and passes the mesh to the same evaluation scripts.
This fixed-pipeline design mirrors a common user workflow: a practitioner selects a general
image-to-3D model and applies it to whatever reference image is available.
8

The robustness rerun addresses a simple concern. If the main experiment used a low
mesh-extraction resolution, then a smaller or thinner mesh might be an artefact of the
extraction grid rather than of the reconstruction itself. Increasing the marching-cubes
resolution cannot prove that no extraction artefact remains, but it can test whether
the qualitative pattern disappears under a denser extraction. In our rerun it does not
disappear. We therefore interpret the higher-resolution run as evidence that the observed
shrinkage is not only a low-resolution extraction effect.

3.4

Metrics

We compute conventional point metrics against the ground-truth object, including Chamfer L2 and F-score at 0.05 and 0.1 thresholds. These metrics are standard in reconstruction
evaluation because they are easy to reproduce and can be applied to sampled point clouds
even when mesh topology differs. Chamfer distance measures average point proximity; Fscore measures how many sampled points fall within a chosen tolerance. Both are useful,
but both are most directly about surface proximity.
We also compute diagnostics aimed at asset usability: predicted mesh volume, zextent, vertex count, and face count. Volume captures whether the reconstruction remains
a three-dimensional body rather than a thin or collapsed surface. Z-extent captures loss of
depth along the view-related axis. Vertex and face counts provide a coarse check on mesh
complexity, although they should not be interpreted as quality measures by themselves.
A dense but collapsed mesh is still a poor asset; a sparse mesh can be acceptable if it
preserves the main volume and shape. For this reason, volume and z-extent receive the
main emphasis in the analysis.
For each stylised input, we report the paired change relative to the photo reconstruction of the same object. Chamfer and F-score are reported as differences, while volume
and z-extent are reported as ratios. A volume ratio below 1 indicates that the stylised
reconstruction has less predicted volume than the paired photo reconstruction. A z-extent
ratio below 1 indicates a shallower reconstruction. We use paired Wilcoxon signed-rank
tests for volume and z-extent differences because the comparisons are within object and
the distributions need not be Gaussian. The statistical tests are not used as the sole
evidence. We interpret them together with effect sizes, mean ratios, and the consistency
of the direction across all five styles.
The main analysis is therefore organised around three questions. First, do stylised
inputs change conventional point metrics relative to photo inputs? Second, do the same
inputs change volumetric and depth-sensitive diagnostics? Third, can these two answers
disagree? The third question is the most important for this paper because it tests whether
a benchmark based only on point metrics would lead to a different conclusion from a
benchmark that also tracks asset-oriented geometry.
We do not normalise away the paired photo reconstruction when reporting the main
diagnostic ratios, because the photo output represents the model’s own baseline under the
9

same object and camera. This choice is conservative for the question we ask. If a model
already reconstructs an object imperfectly from the photo input, the style ratio measures
the additional change caused by stylisation rather than the absolute error of the model.
Absolute metrics against the ground-truth object remain part of the analysis through
Chamfer and F-score, but the paired ratios are more direct for identifying style-induced
collapse.

with a mean volume ratio of 0.257. Low-detail and edge inputs are similarly severe, with

The z-extent results show that this is not only a scale effect. Edge and low-detail
inputs reduce z-extent to roughly 47% and 45% of the photo reconstruction, respectively.
Watercolour reduces z-extent to 63%. Flat colour and sketch preserve more z-extent
on average, but they still lose substantial volume. This pattern suggests several types
of collapse: some styles make the object thinner along depth, while others preserve a
bounding depth but lose bulk or connected volume.
The difference between volume and z-extent is informative. Edge and low-detail inputs
reduce both volume and z-extent, suggesting a depth-compression failure. Sketch inputs,
by contrast, preserve more z-extent on average while producing the lowest volume ratio.
This points to a different failure mode: the reconstruction may maintain a broad depth
range but lose bulk within that range. Flat colour is again distinct. It produces the
mildest z-extent change and the mildest volume reduction, which is consistent with the
idea that flat regions preserve more object-level area information than edge-only or lowdetail inputs. These distinctions would be lost if the analysis reported only a single
aggregate score.

4.2

Point metrics can improve while volumetric usability degrades

The central result is the disagreement between point metrics and volumetric diagnostics.
Sketch improves Chamfer by 0.0100 and F-score@0.05 by 0.0925 relative to the photo
11

input, yet its mean volume ratio is only 0.257. Watercolour and flat inputs show the same
direction: better Chamfer and F-score, lower volume. Even edge improves F-score@0.05
by 0.0564 while reducing volume to one third of the paired photo reconstruction.
This does not mean Chamfer and F-score are wrong. It means they answer a narrower
question. They can reward a reconstruction that places sampled points near the groundtruth visible surface, while missing whether the mesh remains a usable three-dimensional
body. In artistic inputs, simplified contours and reduced texture may make surface alignment easier for some objects, but the model can still infer a thinner or more collapsed
shape.
The paradox is most visible when F-score and volume are read together. All five
stylised inputs improve F-score@0.05 relative to photo in the main experiment. A surfacemetric-only report could therefore describe the stylised inputs as beneficial or at least
harmless. The volume ratios support the opposite interpretation for asset usability. The
reconstructed meshes are substantially smaller, and in several styles shallower as well.
The same output can therefore be closer to the ground-truth surface sample under one
metric while being less useful as an editable 3D body.
One plausible explanation is that stylisation simplifies the evidence available to the
model. A sketch or edge rendering may emphasise the visible contour and suppress texture
that would otherwise create noisy or ambiguous local detail. This can help surface samples
align with the visible object boundary. At the same time, the missing shading and interior
evidence can weaken the model’s inference about thickness and back-side geometry. The
result is a mesh that is efficient at explaining the image from the observed view but
incomplete as a volumetric object. The benchmark does not prove this mechanism directly,
but the style-specific pattern is consistent with it.

4.3

The effect persists at higher mesh-extraction resolution

Table 2 reports the 30-object rerun with mesh extraction at resolution 128. The main
pattern remains. All five stylised inputs reduce predicted volume, with mean ratios from
0.300 for low-detail to 0.588 for flat colour. Edge, low-detail, and watercolour again show
strong z-extent reductions. Sketch still improves F-score@0.05 by 0.0685 while reducing

5.

Discussion

The benchmark points to a practical risk in image-to-3D evaluation. Artistic inputs can
make a reconstruction appear stronger under common point metrics while making the

13

mesh less useful as an object. This matters for computational design and asset generation
because users often care about editability, thickness, and downstream handling, not only
surface proximity.
The result also helps clarify what a benchmark for artistic image-to-3D should measure.
Prior work has shown that specific artistic domains such as sketches, anime portraits, flat
illustrations, and manuscript miniatures are challenging for current methods (Chen et al.,
2023; Cong et al., 2025; Pallotto et al., 2026; Sanghi et al., 2023). Our results suggest
that the evaluation should not stop at whether the generated object looks plausible from
the input view. A method can preserve the outline and still lose the volumetric fact of
the object. Paired photo-versus-style comparisons make that loss visible.
For model developers, the findings suggest several remedies. First, benchmark reports
should include volumetric and thickness-sensitive diagnostics alongside Chamfer and Fscore. Second, style-stripping or geometry-preserving preprocessing should be evaluated
not only by visual similarity but also by whether it restores volume and depth. Third,
model selection for design workflows may need to depend on input style. A fast reconstruction model that is acceptable for photographs may need additional checks before it
is used on sketches or flat illustrations.
The broader implication is that image-to-3D evaluation should be tied to the intended use of the output. If the goal is view synthesis, a source-view or near-view metric
may be adequate. If the goal is an editable mesh, then the output must preserve more
than appearance. It must preserve enough three-dimensional structure to support rotation, editing, texturing, and physical or procedural interpretation. Volume is a simple
diagnostic for this requirement. It is not a complete solution, but it catches a failure
that point metrics can miss. Z-extent provides a second coarse signal, especially for
view-dependent flattening.
The benchmark also suggests a practical screening procedure. Before deploying an
image-to-3D model in a design pipeline, users can test a small paired set of inputs that
represent their actual reference material. The test does not require a large new dataset.
A controlled set of known objects, rendered in the relevant style, can reveal whether the
model is sensitive to abstraction. If the model loses volume under a style that is common
in the workflow, the pipeline can add safeguards: ask for a photo-like reference, run a
style-normalisation step, use a model trained for that input type, or flag the output for
manual inspection.
For researchers developing new image-to-3D systems, the results argue for reporting
paired style robustness rather than only aggregate benchmark scores. A model that
performs well on photographs may still be brittle for sketches. A model that improves Fscore under stylised inputs may still be unsuitable for asset generation if the improvement
comes with volume loss. Reporting both point metrics and asset diagnostics would make
such trade-offs visible. It would also help distinguish true geometric robustness from a
metric improvement caused by simplified contours.

14

The study further shows why controlled synthetic rendering remains useful even when
the target domain is artistic human input. Real drawings and illustrations are important,
but they rarely come with exact 3D ground truth. Controlled stylisation of known 3D
objects cannot replace real artwork, but it provides a bridge between ecological relevance
and measurable geometry. It lets the evaluator ask a question that is difficult to answer
with unpaired data: what changes when only the input style changes?
There is also a methodological lesson for future benchmarks. If a benchmark mixes
many styles and object categories without pairing, the average score may hide where the
model fails. A model could do well on flat illustrations of simple objects and poorly on
edge renderings of thin objects, yet the aggregate number might appear acceptable. The
paired structure used here supports a more diagnostic report. Each object can be treated
as its own control, and each style can be analysed as a perturbation. This structure is
especially useful when the expected failure is not random noise but a directional shift
such as volume loss.
A second lesson concerns metric interpretation. Chamfer distance and F-score should
not be discarded; they remain valuable for measuring sampled surface agreement. The
problem is that they can be over-read. A lower Chamfer value does not necessarily mean
that the generated mesh is a better design asset. It means that the sampled surface is
closer under the chosen sampling and tolerance assumptions. For asset generation, this
statement should be accompanied by diagnostics that describe whether the output still
occupies a plausible three-dimensional region. Volume, z-extent, and related thickness
measures are simple additions that make the interpretation less brittle.
The results also suggest why human inspection can be both necessary and unreliable
as a sole evaluation method. A reviewer looking only at the source view may see a
recognisable object and accept the reconstruction. The failure becomes more obvious
when the mesh is rotated, when a cross-section is inspected, or when the object is used
downstream. A benchmark should therefore make this hidden failure visible numerically
before it reaches a user. Volumetric diagnostics are not a substitute for visual inspection,
but they can decide which outputs deserve closer inspection.

6.

Limitations

This study has several boundaries. We evaluate one primary image-to-3D baseline, TripoSR, so the results should not be stated as universal behaviour across all modern systems.
The benchmark uses 100 curated Objaverse objects, which is sufficient for the observed
paired statistical effect but not for broad category-level claims. A larger benchmark should
stratify objects by shape family, concavity, symmetry, texture dependence, and component count. Such a study could identify which object properties make style-induced collapse
more likely.
The style transformations are controlled proxies for artistic inputs; they do not cover

15

the full diversity of human artwork. Real sketches can include construction lines, perspective errors, intentional exaggeration, ambiguous boundaries, or semantic conventions
that are not reproduced by image filters. Real watercolour and concept art may also
contain stylistic cues that are meaningful to humans but unfamiliar to a reconstruction
model. The controlled proxy is useful for isolating style, but it should be complemented
by studies on real artistic datasets.
The current study also reports mesh-level diagnostics and does not include a human
usability study. Volume and z-extent are interpretable, but they are not the only qualities
that matter. A mesh can preserve volume while having poor topology, disconnected
parts, or unusable surface detail. Conversely, a lower-volume reconstruction might still be
acceptable for a decorative or low-poly asset. Future work should test whether volumetric
diagnostics predict human judgements of editability, recognisability after rotation, and
fabrication readiness.
Finally, the benchmark does not test mitigation strategies. We do not evaluate stylestripping, multi-view prompting, material-aware reconstruction, or newer textured models
as fixes. This is a deliberate scope choice. The first step is to show that the metric gap
exists under a controlled paired design. The next step is to test whether stronger models
or preprocessing methods reduce the gap without sacrificing the benefits of artistic input.

7.

Conclusion

We presented a paired benchmark for measuring how artistic input transformations affect image-to-3D reconstruction when the ground-truth 3D object is known. On 100
curated Objaverse objects reconstructed with TripoSR, five stylised input types all reduce predicted mesh volume relative to photo inputs, while several styles improve Chamfer distance or F-score. A higher-resolution mesh-extraction rerun preserves the same
volume-shrinkage pattern.
The main lesson is that surface proximity and asset usability can disagree. Stylised
inputs may simplify contours in a way that helps point metrics, yet still remove the
evidence needed to infer thickness and volume. For image-to-3D systems used in computational design and 3D asset workflows, evaluation should therefore report volumetric and
thickness-oriented diagnostics together with conventional surface metrics. A reconstruction benchmark should not only ask whether the generated surface is close to the ground
truth. It should also ask whether the output remains a usable three-dimensional object.
